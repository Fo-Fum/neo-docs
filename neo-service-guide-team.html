Neo Café Eatery Introduction
Our Mission
To create special moments and build community — through great food, intentional service, and generous hospitality.

This guide is for everyone at Neo. It's not a rulebook — it's a compass. It gives you a clear picture of what hospitality looks like in practice, why it matters, and what we ask of each other to deliver it.

Service vs Hospitality

These two things are often confused — but they are not the same, and understanding the difference is fundamental to what we do at Neo.

Service is transactional. It's taking an order, delivering food, processing a payment. Hospitality is something deeper. It's the feeling a person carries with them when they leave. It's whether they felt welcome, seen, and cared for — not just accommodated.

At Neo, we are not simply serving brunch. We are curating an experience — one where every customer feels comfortable, cared for, known, and eager to return.

What Exceptional Hospitality Looks Like

Exceptional hospitality is the art of making people feel welcome, seen, and cared for — not just accommodated. It goes beyond meeting expectations to creating moments people remember.

In practice, this means:

Greeting every customer with warmth and full attention — even during peak trade
Remembering regulars by name and knowing their usual order
Scanning the room — noticing when someone looks uncertain and stepping in before they have to ask
Resolving mistakes with generosity and without defensiveness
Caring about every detail — beautifully presented food, tidy menus, clean surfaces, and a space that feels truly looked after

Hospitality is not theatrics. It is consistency in the fundamentals: food, service, and vibe. These done well make the moments that make someone's day.

At its deepest level
Hospitality is an act of love. The great traditions — Greek xenia, Hebrew hesed, Japanese omotenashi — all understood it as something close to sacred: the meeting of the stranger with care.

Neo Café Eatery Section Two · The Customer Journey

What follows is the full arc of a Neo customer experience — from the moment someone arrives to the moment they leave. This is not a script. It's a framework for delivering something memorable at every touchpoint.

1. The Welcome — First Impressions

You have approximately five seconds to acknowledge a customer. Even if it's from across the room, that first moment sets the entire tone for their visit.

Eye contact — give them your full attention
A warm, welcoming smile
A calm and confident tone
Clear direction — especially if there is a wait
Average Service
"Hi guys, how many today? Just wait over there — there's a 15 minute wait on tables."
Neo Hospitality
"Good morning — welcome to Neo. Have you been in before? There's about a 15-minute wait, but we've got you on the list. Stick with us and we'll come and find you when your table's ready."

A good greeting takes more effort upfront. That effort is always worth it. This is the moment a visitor becomes our customer. When done well, they'll queue, they'll trust, and they'll come back.

If there is a wait
Give an honest, realistic timeframe. Update them if the wait extends beyond what was communicated. When there is a queue and empty tables, communicate directly who will be seated when — queuing customers can get anxious.
2. Seating & Drinks Order
Before you call the customer to their table, set it up first: cutlery box, salt and pepper mills, sugar bowl, menus, water and glasses
Once seated, offer drinks straight away, or give them a moment to settle — they'll let you know how to work it
Let the table know about daily specials — deliver with enthusiasm and confidence
Confirm if there are any dietary requirements
Enter the drinks order promptly and before taking another table's order
If you're on Greet & Seat, let whoever's running the Floor know you've sat the table — that you've offered drinks and run through today's Chef and Drinks specials. A quick "Hey, I've just sat 2 on table 1, got their drinks and told them about the specials" keeps the floor aware that coffees are coming and ready to take the next table's order

Body language matters. Use a confident posture and clear, unhurried voice. Your composure tells the customer everything is under control.

3. Taking the Food Order
Once you have delivered drinks, you are ready to take the food order
Know the menu with confidence — ingredients, allergens, and modifications
Make genuine recommendations when asked — your enthusiasm will do the job, even if they don't go with your choice, they'll be more excited about the food
Don't forget to upsell. Even where the menu prompts it, make a recommendation to add to a meal — it lifts the spend per head, but it also opens up dialogue about what pairs well together and shows we're taking the extra time to meet their needs. The power of suggestion goes a long way.
Repeat the order back clearly and confirm any changes and dietary needs
Never guess on ingredients. Never leave an unanswered question without following it up
Remember
A confident, warm recommendation builds trust and makes the decision easier for the customer. Customers look to us for guidance — and that's a privilege.
On timing
Brunch is built on timing. Get it right and the whole experience flows. Lose control of it and the best food in the city won't save the visit. Coffee lands first — always, unless the customer requests otherwise. Communicate delays before the customer feels the need to ask.
4. Checking In

Return to the table one to two minutes after food is served. Issues are far easier to resolve when caught early. This is our last line of defence — we're all human, and occasionally mistakes slip through the cracks. Checking in gives us the chance to fix anything that isn't up to standard, so the customer leaves feeling genuinely looked after. Ask warmly and with care:

The check-in
"Hi — just checking in. How is everything?"
5. Clearing & Second Round Opportunity
Once food has been consumed, this is a great opportunity to offer a second round and something sweet — not just upselling, but being a generous host. "Can I tempt you with another coffee, or something from the cabinet?" Offer with warmth, never with pressure.
Clear plates once the whole table has finished, or when they are being pushed aside
Work efficiently — never empty-handed. If you're heading out, bring something back: an empty water bottle, finished coffees, used plates. But never in a way that feels rushed
Keep water topped up throughout the visit

The details customers notice most are often the ones they couldn't articulate.

6. Closing the Table
Ask once more if there's anything they need and if everything was alright
If you need the table, give them 5 minutes after they've declined anything else, then politely let them know you have guests waiting to be seated
Thank them with sincerity — make eye contact
Invite them to settle up at the counter when they're ready
Reset with care: topped up cutlery box, clean surfaces, properly aligned chairs
The words that matter
"Thanks for coming in — have a great day, and see you next time." To a regular: "See you next week."

7. The Farewell

The till is our final touchpoint — and one of the most overlooked. It is the last impression we leave.

Ask how everything was. If there's an issue, address it. If there's praise, receive it warmly and pass it on to the team.

Find a genuine moment of connection — even in the thirty seconds during payment. If it's a busy service and conversation isn't possible, a warm farewell still matters: "Have a great day — thanks for coming in, and see you next time."

Neo Café Eatery Section Three · Service Standards
Grooming & Presentation

The way we present ourselves is part of the experience. It signals to every customer that we take this seriously — the food, the space, the people. A clean, well-presented team is an expression of respect.

Clean, well-presented uniform at the start of every shift
Hair neat and tidy — away from your face during service
No strong fragrances
Calm, upright posture — confident without being stiff
Table Reset Standard

Every table that's reset must meet the same standard — regardless of how busy service is. Consistency here is one of the clearest signals of the care we take.

Fully wiped down, dry, and completely crumb-free
Cutlery, salt & pepper, and sugar stocked and properly positioned
Clean menus, water, and glasses set for the correct number of customers
Chairs clean and properly aligned
Running & Announcing Food

Know the dish before you pick it up. Place it with intention. Announcing food clearly and confidently is part of the experience — it connects the customer to what they're about to enjoy.

FOH & Kitchen Communication

Neo has a kitchen. Neo has a F.O.H. But Neo only has one team. Different pressures and different rhythms can start to feel like different agendas — and a kitchen-vs-floor divide is one of the most common culture killers in hospitality. The customer doesn't experience "the kitchen" and "the floor" separately. They experience Neo. When something goes wrong, we all carry the result; and when it goes right, it's everyone's win. The way FOH and BOH communicate directly shapes the customer experience — and the culture of Neo. So here's how we keep that one team strong at the pass:

Always get the chef's attention before speaking at the pass: "Hey team, can we talk?"
Clarify everything before you walk away from the pass
Use no-blame language — we succeed and solve problems together
Own your mistakes with the kitchen, fast: "I made an error — can you help me put it right?" The kitchen should be doing the same with you. The Kitchen to F.O.H: "Sorry guys, I missed the bacon on that Neo, it will be out in 2 minutes, can you let the customer know please."
Busy Brunch Standards

Peak service is where Neo's culture is most visible — and most tested. A well-prepared team in a good rhythm actually thrives under pressure. Here's how we do it:

Slow your pace of speech, even when moving quickly — calm is contagious
Communicate proactively and clearly with the kitchen and your team
Stick to the process — it exists precisely for moments like this
Double-check orders before they go in. If you're not sure, confirm
Protect the energy of the room — the customers feel the vibe as much as we do, keep it calm & light, NOT chaotic & stressed

Neo Café Eatery Section Four · Menu Knowledge

Menu knowledge is not about memorising scripts — it's about developing a depth of understanding. Our customers trust us to be the experts. That trust is earned through preparation, curiosity, and care.

Every team member should be able to confidently describe, for every core dish:

The main ingredients and how the dish is prepared
Potential allergens and dietary suitability — including our practices that keep guests with allergies safe
Common modifications — what can and can't be changed
What makes it worth ordering
On allergens — know our kitchen practices
Know that we have a coeliac-friendly deep fryer. Know that allergen-specific dishes are prepared separately on clean equipment. This knowledge isn't a bonus — it's part of your responsibility, and it can genuinely matter to someone's health. When allergens arise, communicate this clearly on the docket. Never guess. Ever.
Non-Negotiable
You must be able to clearly identify: which dishes are gluten-free or adaptable; dairy content across the menu; vegetarian and vegan suitability; what modifications are and are not possible. If you are not certain: "Let me confirm that with the kitchen." Say it without hesitation and follow it up immediately.
Describing a Dish

When a customer asks for a recommendation, this is your moment. A warm, confident description — not a recitation — makes the decision easier and the experience richer. Don't run through the menu like a list. Talk about the food the way you'd talk about something you genuinely love — what it tastes like, what makes it special, why you'd order it yourself.

A customer who feels guided by someone who knows and cares about the food will order with confidence. That confidence is part of the experience.

Coffee & Drinks

All service staff should be able to confidently describe and explain:

Our beans — flavour profile, origin, and the difference between filter (light) and espresso (dark) roast
All milk options and alternative milk upcharges
Standard drink differences — flat white, latte, cappuccino, and beyond
All add-on options and associated charges
Milkshake and smoothie recipes

Menu training takes time to absorb — if you'd like more support in any area, ask your manager. We'd rather you ask than guess.

Neo Café Eatery Section Five · Handling Mistakes

Things go wrong. Even the best teams in the best cafés have off moments. What separates good hospitality from great hospitality is not perfection — it's how generously and honestly we respond when something falls short.

A mistake handled with genuine care can actually strengthen the relationship with a customer. People remember how you made them feel — especially when things went wrong and you stepped up.

The Neo Approach
Acknowledge it immediately — never wait for the customer to raise it twice. Apologise sincerely and without qualification. Fix it as quickly as possible, and communicate what is being done about it. Where appropriate, add a small gesture of goodwill at a manager's discretion.

This isn't about damage control. It's about integrity. A customer who sees you handle a mistake with grace and warmth will trust you more than if nothing had gone wrong at all.

That said, not every situation resolves easily. Some customers are genuinely upset, and they need more than a quick apology. When that happens, work through these four steps:

Question
Ask clarifying questions. Stay calm and don't match their energy. Use relaxed, attentive body language to de-escalate. Make sure you fully understand the complaint — repeating it back to them shows they've been heard, and often takes the heat out of the moment.
Investigate
Follow up immediately with whoever is involved and get to the bottom of what happened.
Resolve
With a manager's support if needed, land on an appropriate fix. If something is wrong with a dish or drink, remake it or offer something else. If they don't want a replacement, refund the item. If the mistake was clearly significant, remake and refund. When in doubt, lean on the side of generosity.
Communicate
Let the customer know what you found and what you're doing about it. At this stage, involve them in the outcome where appropriate — sometimes they'll have a preference, and asking shows respect.

The goal is never to win the exchange. It's to leave the customer feeling that we genuinely cared — and gave them a reason to come back.

Neo Café Eatery Section Six · Scenarios

The following scenarios bring the training to life. This section is not about learning scripts — they are illustrations of how the principles we teach look and feel on the ground. Read them as a team. Discuss them. Ask: what would I do? Where can I improve how I practise hospitality?

Scenario 1 — A Regular Returning

A familiar face walks in. You recognise them, but their name or usual order isn't coming to you right now.

That's okay — recognition doesn't require a perfect memory. Even a warm "Great to see you again" tells someone they matter here, that they're not just another table. That feeling is what keeps people coming back.

But when you do remember — use it. A name, their usual order, a passing reference to something they mentioned last time. These small moments of recognition are quietly powerful. They tell a customer that this place knows them, and that coming here is different from anywhere else.

Over time, this is how a visitor becomes a regular. And regulars are the heartbeat of Neo — they fill the quieter mornings, they bring their friends, and they become part of what makes this place feel like a community, not just a café.

Learn the names. Remember the orders. Notice the people.

Scenario 2 — A Coffee Delay

A customer's coffee is taking longer than expected. They haven't said anything yet — but you can see them waiting, and you've noticed.

Don't wait for them to come to you. Go to the table, acknowledge it directly, and own it: "Your coffee is taking a little longer than usual — I'm so sorry for the wait. It's on its way and I'll follow it up right now."

Then do exactly that. Check in with the barista immediately and get a realistic timeframe. If the delay is significant enough to affect meal timing, let the kitchen know. Read the table — some customers will want to wait and have everything together; others will just want to get on with their meal. A simple "Would you like us to go ahead and bring your food out, or would you prefer to wait for your coffee?" puts them in control.

The coffee delay isn't the problem. Being left in the dark is.

Scenario 3 — A Celebration at the Table

You notice a birthday card on the table, or someone mentions it's a special occasion.

We are here to create moments — not just to deliver meals. A small, thoughtful gesture can leave a lasting impression. This might be a complimentary sweet, a personal message passed from the kitchen, or simply a warm acknowledgement. You don't need to make a fuss — just make it feel noticed.

Scenario 4 — Families with Young Children

A family walks in with young children in tow. Before they've even sat down, there's an opportunity to set the tone for their whole visit.

If they're waiting for a table, acknowledge the children first — a smile, a quick word. Reassure the parents of when they'll be seated. A family managing a pushchair, a restless toddler, and a queue is already working hard — let them know they're in good hands.

Once they're being seated:

Ask if they need a kids chair or bumbo seat before they have to ask themselves
Bring the kids menu and colouring pens to the table before anything else — this buys everyone a moment to breathe
Check whether the children should eat first — many families will want this, and offering shows you understand how their visit actually works
Let the parents know there are quick, easy options in the cabinet if the kids get restless
Point out the change station and any toys or play features (on B2)
Offer to help store prams or pushchairs, and clear any hazards from the table if asked

The details here matter more than in almost any other scenario. Parents with young children are quietly hoping this was a good idea. Our job is to make it feel like it was.

Make it easy. Make it warm. Make it worth the effort of getting out the door.

Scenario 5 — A Frustrated Customer

A customer calls you over mid-service. Their food arrived later than expected, and when it came, one dish wasn't quite right. They're not shouting — but they're clearly unhappy, and the way they're speaking to you is sharp.

The first thing to do is nothing dramatic. Don't become defensive, and don't over-apologise in a way that feels hollow. Take a breath, hold your composure, and give them your full attention. Face them squarely, stay relaxed, and let them finish.

Acknowledge what they've experienced: "I completely understand — that's not the experience we want for you, and I'm sorry." Then ask the right question: "Can I ask what would make this right for you?" Often, people just want to feel heard.

If the dish isn't right, offer to remake it or let them choose something else
If they've lost confidence in the meal entirely, involve a manager and consider a partial or full refund
If they remain upset despite your best efforts, bring a manager in calmly: "I've spoken with this guest about their experience — I'd love for you to step in."

After the situation is resolved, check back in before they leave. A simple "I just wanted to make sure everything was better from there" closes the loop and shows genuine care.

The hardest moments, handled well, leave the strongest impressions.

Scenario 6 — Asking a Finished Table to Move On

The café is full and there's a queue out the door. A table has finished their meals and declined anything further.

Don't rush straight to the ask. First, top up their water — it signals that we're not in the business of rushing people. Give them five minutes. Then return with a genuine offer: another coffee, something from the cabinet, a sweet to finish. Make it real — not a formality, not a countdown. If the answer is still no, it's time to gently move things along.

Approach with a warm smile: "I hope you've had a lovely time with us today — it's been great having you in. I just wanted to give you a heads up that we have quite a few guests waiting for a table. Whenever you're ready, we'd love to free this one up for them — absolutely no rush, just whenever it suits you. Thanks so much for understanding."

Keep it light. In most cases, once guests register the queue at the door, they'll make the decision themselves.

The goal is for them to leave feeling good about Neo — not guilty for staying, and not rushed out the door.

A Final Word
The Standard,
Every Shift.

This guide covers a lot of ground. But if there's one thing that ties all of it together, it's this: the standard doesn't change based on how busy it is, how tired you are, or how difficult the morning has been. It stays the same from Moment 1 to Moment Done. That consistency is what people feel — even when they can't articulate why they keep coming back.

Peak service is where Neo's culture is most visible — and most tested. When pressure builds, come back to the basics:

Consistency — the same care and attention, every table, every shift
Warmth — not performed, but genuine
Precision — the details done well, every time
Ownership — stepping up without being asked
Pride — in the work, the team, and the space we hold for our customers

We do not aim for adequate. We aim for something people remember and return for. That's a high bar — and it's exactly where it should be.

Thank you for being part of what makes Neo worth coming back to.
